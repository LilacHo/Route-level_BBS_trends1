BBS route-level trends, 2010–2025 · anthro model · RCP 4.5

Do SDM categories predict observed population trends?

A mixed model with heavy-tailed (Student-t) errors, fitted to 213,127 route trends from 468 species, explained step by step for readers who haven't used mixed models.

Models fitted with glmmTMB in R 4.6 · Code: 4f_sdm_category_mixed_models.R (main analysis), 4g_sdm_category_robustness.R (sensitivity checks) · Report prepared 30 September 2026

Short answer

Yes, in the predicted direction, but the effect is small and differs a lot between species.

For the average species, routes in areas the SDM classifies as Contraction trend 0.76 %/yr worse than that species' routes in Expansion areas: Contraction − Expansion = −0.76 %/yr (95% CI −1.03 to −0.49, p < 0.001). The category explains under 1% of the variation in trends within a species, and roughly a third of species show the opposite pattern. By bird group, the effect is strongest in eastern forests, boreal forests and grasslands, and absent in water-associated groups.

1The question

For each species, a species distribution model (SDM) projects how climate suitability changes by 2025 and classifies every location into one of seven categories. We group these into three:

  • Contraction: extirpation, worsening, or slightly worsening (codes 1–3)
  • Stable: neutral (code 4)
  • Expansion: slightly improving, improving, or colonization (codes 5–7)

If the SDM captures something real, a species should be declining faster on routes in its Contraction areas and doing better on routes in its Expansion areas. The question is whether the observed BBS trends, 2010–2025, show that pattern. Throughout, the key number is Contraction − Expansion, which the hypothesis predicts to be negative.

2The data

Each row is one BBS route for one species.

  • Response: the route's estimated population trend in % per year. It comes from the route-level spatial (iCAR) model that includes the anthropogenic land-use covariate: trend = 100 × (exp(βroute) − 1), using the posterior mean of the route's slope.
  • Predictor: the SDM category (RCP 4.5) at the route's location, grouped into the three levels above.
  • Grouping variable: the species.

We started with 259,490 route × species rows from 469 species files. All passed the per-route convergence check (route_converged). We then dropped 46,363 rows in category 0 ("never suitable") or with no SDM value, because the hypothesis makes no prediction there. That leaves 213,127 routes from 468 species. 439 of these species have routes in at least two categories, and only those species can tell us how trends differ between categories within a species.

On its face this already looks like a strong confirmation: a gap of about 2 %/yr between Contraction and Expansion routes. The next section explains why we can't take it at face value.

3Why a simple comparison misleads

Comparing the three averages above, or running a Kruskal–Wallis test on all routes, treats the 213,127 routes as 213,127 independent observations of one shared relationship. Two things break that assumption.

Problem 1: species differ, and categories are not spread evenly across species

Some species are increasing almost everywhere; others are declining almost everywhere. If species that happen to be doing well also happen to have many Expansion routes, the Expansion average will be high even if no single species does better in its Expansion areas. This is a composition effect, the bird version of Simpson's paradox. It says nothing about the hypothesis, which is about differences within a species.

Problem 2: routes of the same species are not independent

Two routes of the same species share that species' biology, its range-wide drivers, and the same fitted model. They are much more alike than two routes of different species. Treating them as independent makes the sample look far larger than it really is, so standard errors come out too small and p-values too optimistic. A species with 2,000 routes also outweighs one with 20 routes by 100 to 1.

Problem 3: a few routes have extreme trends

Most route trends fall within ±12 %/yr, but a few hundred lie beyond ±20 %/yr. Methods that assume normally distributed scatter give these rare routes more pull on the averages than they deserve.

What we actually want to know: for a typical species, how does its trend change as we move from its Contraction areas to its Expansion areas, and how consistent is that across species? A mixed model with heavy-tailed errors addresses all three problems.

4The model, in plain terms

Picture fitting a separate small regression for each species: trend explained by category, using only that species' routes. Each fit gives three numbers:

  • the species' trend in its Stable areas (its baseline);
  • how much lower or higher its Contraction routes are than that baseline;
  • how much lower or higher its Expansion routes are than that baseline.

Then average those numbers across species. The average of the last two answers our question, and their spread across species tells us how consistent the pattern is.

A mixed model does exactly this in one fit, with two improvements over literally fitting 468 regressions (Problems 1 and 2):

  • It weights species sensibly. A species with thousands of routes gets a precise estimate, but it still counts as one species when we average; it doesn't swamp the result.
  • It borrows strength for data-poor species. A species with only 3 Contraction routes has a very noisy Contraction estimate. The model pulls that estimate part of the way toward the across-species average, more when a species has little data and hardly at all when it has lots. This is called partial pooling or shrinkage.

For Problem 3, the leftover route-to-route scatter is modelled with a Student-t distribution instead of a normal one. A Student-t is bell-shaped like the normal but has heavier tails, set by its degrees of freedom (ν): the smaller ν, the heavier the tails. Under a Student-t, an extreme route is "expected occasionally", so it pulls the estimates much less. The model estimates ν from the data.

The equation

For route i belonging to species s:

trendi = (β₀ + u₀ₛ) + (βC + uC,s) × Contractioni + (βE + uE,s) × Expansioni + εi, εi ~ Student-t(ν, σ)

Contractioni and Expansioni are 0/1 indicators. A Stable route has both equal to 0, so Stable is the reference level that the other two are compared against.

TermKindMeaning
β₀fixed effectTrend in Stable areas for the average species
βCfixed effectAverage species: Contraction routes minus Stable routes
βEfixed effectAverage species: Expansion routes minus Stable routes
u₀ₛrandom effectHow far species s's baseline sits from the average ("random intercept")
uC,s, uE,srandom effectsHow far species s's own Contraction and Expansion effects sit from the average ("random slopes")
εiresidualRoute-to-route scatter left over within a species
ν, σresidual parametersTail heaviness (degrees of freedom) and scale of that scatter

Fixed effects are the population-average numbers we report and test. Random effects are the species-specific deviations. We don't estimate each one as a free parameter. Instead, the model assumes they come from a normal distribution centred on zero, estimates the spread of that distribution (a standard deviation for each), and lets the three deviations for a species be correlated. That assumption is what produces the partial pooling described above. Only the route-level residual is Student-t; the species-level deviations are still assumed normal.

Because the error distribution is not normal, this is a generalized linear mixed model (GLMM). The link is the identity, so every coefficient is still in %/yr. And because the Student-t is symmetric, the fixed effects still describe the average species' average trend, just estimated in a way that isn't dominated by a few extreme routes.

How cat3 becomes two 0/1 columns. cat3 is a factor with levels Stable, Contraction, Expansion, and Stable is listed first. R turns a three-level factor into an intercept plus two indicator columns; the first level is represented by "both are 0":

What (1 + cat3 | species_code) adds. Read the bar as "the terms on the left, separately for each species on the right". The left side repeats the same three columns as the fixed part, so each fixed coefficient gets a species-specific partner: β₀ gets u₀ₛ, βC gets uC,s, βE gets uE,s. That pairing is why the equation groups them in brackets. The model estimates the three SDs and three correlations of these deviations; the individual deviations come out afterwards with ranef(), and each species' own coefficients (fixed + random) with coef().

The residual is never written in the formula. family = t_family() tells glmmTMB to use a Student-t for it; the default would be normal.

5How the model was fitted

  1. Build the data. Read every *_anthro_route_trends_sdm.csv, keep converged routes, drop category 0 and missing values, and recode rcp45 into the three-level factor cat3 with Stable as the reference level.
  2. Fit the model with the glmmTMB call above. glmmTMB estimates everything, including ν and σ, by maximum likelihood.
  3. Check the fit is usable. The optimiser converged and the Hessian (the curvature of the likelihood at the optimum) is positive definite. That means every parameter, including the variances, is identified and its standard error can be trusted.
  4. Check the error distribution (section 6): do the residuals actually follow the fitted Student-t?
  5. Form the key contrast. The model reports each category against Stable. Contraction minus Expansion is βC − βE; the hypothesis predicts it is negative. Its standard error comes from the estimated covariance of the two coefficients, written as √(L V Lᵀ) with L = (0, 1, −1), because the two estimates are not independent.
  6. Get p-values. glmmTMB reports Wald z-tests (estimate ÷ SE, compared with a standard normal). The population-level effects are estimated from about 440 species, so this approximation is safe.
  7. Extract species-level estimates. coef(fit)$cond$species_code returns each species' own intercept and effects (fixed effect plus that species' random effect). These give Figure 2.

6Checking the error distribution

If the fitted Student-t is the right description of the route-to-route scatter, the share of routes beyond its 1% and 0.1% cutoffs should be close to 1% and 0.1%.

Share of routes beyond the fitted Student-t's cutoffExpectedObserved
1% cutoff (two-sided)1%0.88%
0.1% cutoff (two-sided)0.1%0.05%

The observed shares are close to the expected 1% and 0.1%, and if anything slightly below at the extreme end, so the Student-t captures the tails well. The estimated ν = 3.4 means very heavy tails: extreme routes are far more common than a normal distribution would allow, which is why the model uses Student-t errors.

The correlations among the three species-level effects are all weak (between −0.02 and +0.19). A species' overall trend tells you little about how strongly it follows the SDM.

If species' Contraction − Expansion effects are normally distributed with mean −0.76 and SD 2.37, about 37% of species should have a true effect above zero, the reverse of the prediction. The species-level estimates agree (Figure 2): 241 of the 365 species with both Contraction and Expansion routes (66%) have a negative estimate, a little more than expected because shrinkage pulls data-poor species toward the negative average. A model-free check points the same way: among the 290 species with at least 3 routes in each of those two categories, 63% have a higher raw mean trend in Expansion.

How much does the category predict?

A reliable average effect is not the same as a strong predictor. Remove each species' own mean trend and ask how much of the remaining route-to-route variation the SDM category explains: R² = 0.006, or 0.6%. For comparison, knowing only which species a route belongs to explains about 25% of the variation in trends. The within-species signal is real but small next to the other things that make one route's trend differ from another's. (These R² values are computed directly from the data, not from the model.)

9By bird group

Each species belongs to one of 12 habitat groups (eastern forests, grasslands, marshlands, and so on). Arctic is left out: it has a single species with two routes, both Stable, so it offers no category contrast. That leaves 11 groups.

The group model

Species remain the random-effects unit, exactly as in the main model, and each group gets its own average category effects as fixed effects. Let g(s) be the habitat group of species s:

fit_g trendi = (β0,g(s) + u₀ₛ) + (βC,g(s) + uC,s) × Contractioni + (βE,g(s) + uE,s) × Expansioni + εi, εi ~ Student-t(ν, σ)
glmmTMB(trend ~ 0 + group + group:cat3 + (1 + cat3 | species_code),
        family = t_family())

Compared with the main model, each fixed coefficient now carries the subscript g(s): every group has its own Stable-area baseline (β0,g, from 0 + group) and its own Contraction and Expansion effects (βC,g, βE,g, from group:cat3). 0 + removes the global intercept so each group's baseline is estimated directly rather than as an offset from a reference group. (1 + cat3 | species_code) is unchanged, so every group's estimate is still an average over its species, not its routes. Group enters as a fixed effect rather than a random one because we want a separate estimate for each group, and 11 groups is too few to estimate a between-group variance reliably. Each group's Contraction − Expansion difference is βC,g − βE,g.

Do groups differ?

The 11 group estimates always look somewhat different, because each carries noise. To test whether the differences are real, we compare fit_g with a null model in which groups keep their own baselines but share one set of category effects:

fit_g0 trendi = (β0,g(s) + u₀ₛ) + (βC + uC,s) × Contractioni + (βE + uE,s) × Expansioni + εi, εi ~ Student-t(ν, σ)
glmmTMB(trend ~ 0 + group + cat3 + (1 + cat3 | species_code),
        family = t_family())

The only difference is the subscript g(s) on βC and βE. fit_g0 has 13 fixed effects (11 baselines + 2 shared category effects); fit_g has 33 (11 baselines + 22 group-specific category effects). The random effects, σ and ν are the same in both. A likelihood-ratio test asks whether the 20 extra parameters improve the fit more than chance would: twice the difference in log-likelihood, compared with a χ² distribution on 20 degrees of freedom. Both models are fitted by maximum likelihood, which this comparison requires.

Result: χ² = 66.7, df = 20, p = 6 × 10⁻⁷, and fit_g's AIC is lower by 26.7. The category effect genuinely differs among groups. fit_g0 serves only as this reference point; all group estimates come from fit_g.

Group explains only a small part of how species differ, though. In fit_g0, each species' category effects deviate from the single shared βC and βE; in fit_g, from their own group's. Moving from one to the other shrinks the species-level spread in the Contraction effect from SD 1.61 to 1.50, and in the Expansion effect from 1.87 to 1.79: about 8–13% of the variance. Most of the heterogeneity is between species within the same group.

Reading the group results

  • Clear support in three groups: eastern forests (−1.55), boreal forests (−1.53) and grasslands (−1.39), about twice the all-species average. Boreal forests rest on only 299 Expansion routes, hence the wide interval.
  • Suggestive but not significant in generalists (−0.70) and western forests (−0.64): both have p ≈ 0.05 on their own, but not after correcting for 11 tests. Aridlands (−0.42) shows no clear association.
  • No detectable effect in the three water-associated groups (waterbirds, marshlands, coastal), nor in subtropical forests or urban/suburban species. Their estimates lean slightly the "wrong" way (positive), but none comes close to significance. One possible explanation is that wetland and water birds track water availability more closely than the climate variables behind the SDMs. This analysis doesn't test that.
  • "No detectable effect" is not the same as "no effect". Coastal, urban/suburban and subtropical estimates rest on few species or few routes, and their intervals are compatible with effects as large as the strongest groups'.

10Conclusion

  • The direction matches the hypothesis. Within the average species, trends run Contraction < Stable < Expansion. The Contraction − Expansion gap is −0.76 %/yr (95% CI −1.03 to −0.49, p < 0.001).
  • The effect is asymmetric. Expansion areas stand out clearly (+0.55 vs Stable); Contraction areas only slightly (−0.21 vs Stable, p = 0.027).
  • The result is robust. Precision weighting, the full 7-level gradient and a model-free sign test all point the same way with similar size.
  • The pattern is not universal. Species vary around the average by much more than the average itself (SD 2.37 vs mean −0.76). About two-thirds of species follow the prediction and one-third go the other way.
  • The effect differs by bird group. It is clearest in eastern forests, boreal forests and grasslands (−1.4 to −1.6 %/yr) and undetectable in the water-associated groups. Group accounts for only a small share of the variation between species, however; species within the same group still differ widely.
  • It is a weak predictor at the route level. The category explains about 0.6% of within-species trend variation. It supports the SDMs at the level of the typical species, but it is not a useful forecast for any particular route or species.

11Caveats

  • Trend uncertainty is not carried through. Each route contributes its posterior mean trend, and its own uncertainty is ignored. The precision-weighted check gave the same answer. Fitting the category effect inside the Stan model would propagate uncertainty fully.
  • Neighbouring routes are correlated. The iCAR model smooths trends across neighbouring routes, and SDM categories come in spatial blocks, so routes within a species are not independent. This mostly affects the precision of individual species' estimates. The population-average result draws its uncertainty from variation between species, so it is much less affected.
  • Species-level effects are still assumed normal. The Student-t handles extreme routes, not extreme species. Figure 2's long tail suggests a few species with very strong effects, which may inflate the between-species SDs somewhat.
  • Range position is a possible confounder. Expansion areas often lie at the poleward or upslope edge of a range. Part of the effect could reflect range position rather than climate suitability as such. Adding each route's position relative to its species' range centre would test this.
  • The timing differs. The SDM categories describe projected change to 2025; the trends describe what happened 2010–2025. The test asks whether projected suitability change lines up with recent population change, not whether one causes the other.

12Glossary

Mixed model
A regression with both fixed effects (population-average relationships) and random effects (group-specific deviations drawn from a common distribution). "Mixed" refers to having both.
LMM vs GLMM
A linear mixed model assumes normally distributed errors. A generalized LMM allows other error distributions (Poisson, binomial, negative binomial, Student-t) and, where needed, a link function. The main model here is a GLMM with Student-t errors and an identity link.
Student-t errors
A residual distribution shaped like the normal but with heavier tails, set by its degrees of freedom (ν). Low ν (here 3.4) means extreme values are expected occasionally, so they pull the estimates less. As ν grows, the Student-t becomes the normal.
Random intercept
Each group (species) gets its own baseline level, estimated as a deviation from the overall intercept.
Random slope
Each group gets its own version of a predictor's effect, estimated as a deviation from the average effect.
Partial pooling / shrinkage
Group-specific estimates are pulled toward the overall average, more strongly for groups with little data. It sits between "complete pooling" (ignore groups) and "no pooling" (a separate fit per group).
Maximum likelihood
Estimation by choosing the parameter values under which the observed data are most probable. glmmTMB uses it by default; it also makes AIC and likelihood-ratio comparisons between models valid.
AIC
Akaike information criterion: a measure of fit that penalises extra parameters. Lower is better; differences above about 10 are usually treated as decisive.
Likelihood-ratio test
Compares two nested models (one a simplified version of the other) by how much better the fuller model fits, relative to the extra parameters it uses.
